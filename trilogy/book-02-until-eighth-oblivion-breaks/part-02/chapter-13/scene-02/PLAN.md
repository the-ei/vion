# Scene Plan: The Case

## Summary

David walks Ruth through the transactions. His firm provided financial services to entities that later turned out to be connected to actors involved in Book 1's systems failures. The connections are real but indirect - three or four degrees of separation. The firm didn't know what the money would ultimately support; they processed legitimate-seeming transactions. But "didn't know" is a legal position being contested. The investigation is politically charged - a Democratic prosecutor building a career on holding finance accountable for tech disasters. David's firm isn't the biggest fish but they're the most vulnerable.

## Key Elements

- David's explanation of the transactions: technical financial detail
- The degrees of separation: indirect but real connections
- The "didn't know" defense: its legal merit and ethical ambiguity
- The prosecutor's political motivation: career-building through targeting
- Why David's firm is vulnerable: not the biggest but the weakest
- Ruth's professional analysis running beneath her maternal concern
- The specific financial instruments involved
- The question: legal guilt vs. moral complicity

## Characters

- **Ruth Abramson (POV):** Her legal mind analyzing the case while her heart fears for her son. The professional assessment is how she processes emotional overwhelm.
- **David Abramson:** Explaining his situation, defensive but also genuinely confused about where legitimate business ended and culpability began.

## Setting

- David's living room: documents spread, screens showing financial flows
- The apartment as office, the domestic space invaded by legal crisis

## Timeline

- **Date:** Mid-July 2034
- **Time:** Evening, approximately 5:30-7:30 PM
- **Duration:** Two hours

## Page Target

5 pages (~1,375 words)

## Stylistic Notes

Technical financial language rendered accessible but not oversimplified. Ruth's dual consciousness: legal analyst and worried mother. The scene should make clear that David isn't simply guilty or innocent - the categories don't map cleanly. Knausgaard in the accumulated detail of explanation; Carson in the moments where legal language fails to capture what actually happened.
