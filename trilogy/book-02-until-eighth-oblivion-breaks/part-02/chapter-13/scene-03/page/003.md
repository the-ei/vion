"Even if the person being punished isn't the person most responsible?"

"Even then. Because the person most responsible is probably a system, not an individual, and we don't have mechanisms for punishing systems. We only have mechanisms for punishing people. So we punish the people we can reach and hope that creates enough deterrence to change the systems."

Ruth leaned back in the hotel chair, feeling the weight of her daughter's argument settle over her. Rebecca was right, in a way that was hard to argue against. The law had failed to adapt to distributed harm, to systemic complicity, to the modern architecture of responsibility-avoidance. In the absence of better tools, it used the tools it had.

"He's your brother," Ruth said. "Does that change anything?"

"It makes me sad. It makes me angry at him for getting into this position, angry at the system for making this position possible, angry at myself for not being able to just support him unconditionally the way you do. But it doesn't change what I think is true."

"Your father used to say that truth and love were supposed to go together."

"Dad was an optimist." Rebecca's voice softened. "Mom, I do love David. I'm not saying I want him destroyed. I'm saying that loving him doesn't mean pretending he's innocent. It means acknowledging what he did, being present for the consequences."

"That's what I'm trying to do."

"I know. And I think you're doing it better than I could. Just - don't let your love for him blind you to what this situation actually is."

They said goodbye. Ruth sat in the silence of the hotel room, her daughter's words settling around her like ash.
