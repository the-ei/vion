# Scene Plan: What Holds

## Summary

Evening at Ruth's home. She grades papers, eats leftover soup alone, and calls her son David in New York. His world is different from Rebecca's: markets recovered quickly, his fund performed well during the volatility, he's thinking about buying a vacation property. Ruth loves her son but struggles to reconcile his comfort with her disquiet - he inhabits the system that produced the crisis and sees only its resilience. After they hang up, Ruth can't sleep. The final pages shift to Carson mode - shorter sentences, more white space - as she stands at her window watching the Berkeley fog roll in. A meditation on what endures when faith erodes. She thinks of the cases she ruled on, whether they mattered. She thinks of Susan, who would have known what to say. The chapter ends with dawn approaching, Ruth still at the window, the op-ed still unfinished.

## Key Elements

- Evening domestic routine: grading, simple food, the solitude of widowhood
- Phone call with David: love across political/economic divide
- David's financial world: the crisis as opportunity, markets as self-healing
- Ruth's dissonance: her son's comfort versus her knowledge of harm
- Sleeplessness as chronic condition since Susan's death
- The window meditation: fog as metaphor, the city below
- Reflections on Ruth's judicial career: the cases, the rulings, the question of impact
- Susan's presence in absence: what she would say, the companionship Ruth lacks
- The unfinished op-ed as symbol: Ruth's inability to articulate what she knows
- Dawn arriving: a day survived, nothing resolved

## Characters

- **Ruth Abramson (POV):** Central consciousness; alone with doubt and memory
- **David Abramson (phone):** Ruth's son, finance worker, voice of the comfortable classes
- **Susan Abramson (memory):** Ruth's late wife, mourned throughout

## Setting

- **Location:** Ruth's home in Berkeley - kitchen, study, bedroom, window overlooking the city
- **Physical details:** Evening light fading, fog rolling in, the quiet of a house meant for two
- **Atmosphere:** Intimate, melancholic, philosophical

## Timeline

- **Date:** Late February 2034
- **Time:** Approximately 6:00 PM to 5:00 AM (next morning)
- **Duration:** 11 hours (mostly sleepless)
- **Relation to chapter:** Closes the chapter, brings Ruth's day and doubts to rest

## Page Target

4 pages (~1,100 words)

## Stylistic Notes

- **Mode:** Shift from Knausgaard (evening routine) to Carson (sleepless meditation)
- **Sentence structure:** Increasingly short and fragmented toward dawn
- **Interiority emphasis:** Ruth's mind ranging across career, marriage, the nature of faith
- **Key techniques:** White space as meaning; grief made structural; the chapter fading rather than ending
