# Scene Plan: Static

## Summary

Jerome Washington wakes at 6:00 AM to silence. His phone, which usually shows overnight notifications, emails, breaking news - shows nothing. No internet connection. He tries to refresh. Nothing. He checks the wifi router - it blinks red. He restarts it. Still red. His laptop won't connect. The TV shows static on channels that should be streaming. For a man whose entire life is organized around information flow, the sudden absence is disorienting, almost physical. He goes through increasingly archaic troubleshooting: unplugging devices, checking cable connections, finally finding a small battery-powered radio in a kitchen drawer. The radio works. And what he hears on the radio - fragmentary reports of widespread outages, confused announcers, dead air - tells him this isn't his equipment. This is something larger.

## Key Elements

- The silence of disconnection: Jerome's devices as extensions of his mind, now severed
- Information professional confronting information void
- Escalating troubleshooting: the ritual attempts to restore normality
- The radio as last medium standing: return to an older technology
- Fragmentary radio reports: hints of the scope without clarity
- Jerome's identity as journalist: who is he without access to information?
- Baltimore apartment setting: domestic space become isolation chamber
- Knausgaard attention to the specific procedures of troubleshooting

## Characters

- **Jerome Washington (POV):** Investigative journalist waking to silence
- **Denise Washington (asleep initially):** His wife, waking as Jerome's troubleshooting grows more frantic
- **Radio voices:** Anonymous broadcasters, the only information source

## Setting

- **Jerome and Denise's Baltimore apartment:** The devices that usually connect them to the world, now dead
- **Kitchen drawer:** Where the old battery radio was stored, forgotten
- **The radio's audio:** Fragmentary news, static, the auditory landscape of crisis

## Stylistic Notes

Begin in Knausgaard mode: the physical process of waking, the habitual reach for the phone, the growing unease when nothing responds. The mundane actions of troubleshooting rendered in detail - unplugging, waiting, plugging back in - become strange rituals. Carson elements enter with the radio: the fragments of information, the static between words, the gaps that can't be filled. Let the prose itself become more broken as the scene progresses.
