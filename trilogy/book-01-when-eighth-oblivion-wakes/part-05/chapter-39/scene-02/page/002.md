"Judge Abramson, thank you for joining us." The senator, a woman Ruth vaguely remembered from confirmation hearings years ago, opened with the expected courtesies. "We're hoping you can help us understand what regulatory frameworks might be appropriate for advanced AI systems. What would you suggest we do?"

The question was so broad as to be almost unanswerable. Ruth had expected this—had prepared for it, even—but the gap between the question and any meaningful response still struck her as vast.

"With respect, Senator, I think the framing of the question may be part of the problem. You're asking what regulatory frameworks might be appropriate, which assumes that regulation is the right tool for this challenge. I'm not sure it is."

The faces around the table registered varying degrees of discomfort. These were people whose work was regulation, whose entire professional existence depended on the assumption that problems could be solved through legislative and administrative action.

"Could you elaborate, Judge?"

"Regulation works when you can define what you're regulating, when you can measure compliance, when the thing being regulated changes slowly enough for rules to keep up with it. None of those conditions apply to AI development as it currently exists. The capabilities are advancing faster than any regulatory body could track. The systems themselves are increasingly difficult for their own creators to understand. And the fundamental nature of what we're regulating—what intelligence means, what agency means, what safety means in this context—is itself in flux."

The senator was taking notes, but her expression suggested she had hoped for something more actionable.
