Yusuf had delivered to both neighborhoods. He had carried organic groceries to the Kenwood doors, handed them to people who barely looked at him before closing their doors. He had carried bulk goods to the Lyndale apartments, exchanged greetings with people who knew his name because he had been delivering to them for three years.

The algorithm did not see this geography. To the app, every delivery was the same - a pickup point, a drop point, an optimal route between them, a problem in logistics to be solved. The human differences were invisible, reduced to addresses and efficiency metrics, the texture of lives flattened into data points that could be processed.

But the crisis made them visible again. The system's failure had stripped away the illusion of sameness, revealed the vastly different stakes that different people faced when the infrastructure stopped working.

He was stopped at a light that wasn't working, waiting his turn in the improvised four-way, when he saw a woman at a bus stop. She was older, maybe his mother's age, and she was crying - not dramatically, just quiet tears rolling down her face as she stared at her phone.

Yusuf pulled over.

"Are you okay?"

She looked up, startled. "My phone stopped working. The bus app isn't running. I don't know how to get home."

"Where do you live?"

"Cedar Riverside. I was supposed to be there an hour ago. My grandchildren are waiting."

Cedar Riverside was maybe fifteen minutes from here, close to his own neighborhood, one of the places in the city where people like him had made homes. The woman was Somali - he could tell from her accent, from the way she wrapped her hijab, from the particular Somali cadence of her worry. She might even know his mother from the community, from the mosque, from the networks of connection that predated and would outlast any app.

"I can take you," he said. "I'm heading that direction anyway."

"You would do that? But I don't have any money for - "

"It's okay. No charge. Wallahi, it's fine."
